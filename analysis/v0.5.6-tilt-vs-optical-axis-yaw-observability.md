# v0.5.6-tilt-vs-optical-axis-yaw-observability

## Request

Clarify the statement:

```text
In real systems, the pose component around the viewing direction is often less reliable than tilt.
```

and answer:

- why say that?
- what is the geometric basis?
- is it always true?

## Short Answer

It is **not universally true**.

The defensible version is:

- in many forward-looking or center-dominated viewing setups, especially with weak initialization or coupled BA, the rotation **around the current optical axis** is often less observable than tilt;
- but if the image has many wide off-axis features, strong texture, or strong external orientation priors, that gap can shrink or disappear.

So this is a geometry-dependent statement, not a universal law.

## Most Important Geometric Basis

Under the equidistant projection used in this repository:

```text
u = cx + f * theta * cos(phi)
v = cy + f * theta * sin(phi)
```

Let the optical-axis yaw perturbation be a small in-plane spin `delta_psi`.

In this project's convention, that mostly perturbs `phi`, so:

```text
delta_phi ≈ delta_psi
```

Then the first-order image sensitivity is:

```text
du/dpsi = -f * theta * sin(phi) = -(v - cy)
dv/dpsi =  f * theta * cos(phi) =  (u - cx)
```

Therefore the pixel motion magnitude caused by optical-axis yaw is:

```text
sqrt((du/dpsi)^2 + (dv/dpsi)^2) = f * theta = r
```

That is the key result:

- the image effect of optical-axis yaw is proportional to image radius `r`;
- near the principal point, `r -> 0`;
- so near-axis features carry very weak first-order evidence for optical-axis spin.

## Why Tilt Is Different

Consider a point initially on the optical axis:

```text
q = [0, 0, 1]^T
```

A small pitch perturbation `delta_beta` gives approximately:

```text
q' ≈ [delta_beta, 0, 1]^T
```

which projects to:

```text
delta u ≈ f * delta_beta
delta v ≈ 0
```

Likewise a small roll perturbation `delta_alpha` gives:

```text
delta u ≈ 0
delta v ≈ -f * delta_alpha
```

So even for a near-axis feature:

- tilt produces a first-order pixel displacement;
- optical-axis yaw produces almost no first-order displacement.

This is the cleanest mathematical reason why the viewing-axis spin can be less observable than tilt in center-dominated imagery.

## What This Means Physically

Near the optical axis:

- changing tilt pushes points radially across the image;
- changing optical-axis yaw mostly spins points around the principal point.

If a point is already very close to the principal point:

- radial motion from tilt is still visible;
- rotational motion around the center is tiny.

So the statement is not just heuristic. It follows directly from the local image sensitivity.

## When The Statement Is Reasonable

This "yaw less reliable than tilt" claim is most reasonable in scenes or pipelines with:

- many observations concentrated near the optical axis;
- forward-looking motion and weak parallax;
- short local BA windows;
- weak initialization or recovery after tracking loss;
- coupled pose-depth / pose-translation / pose-structure optimization.

In those regimes, the optical-axis spin is easier to mis-explain or to absorb into other variables.

## When It Is Not A Good General Claim

The statement should **not** be read as universally true.

It weakens or can fail when:

- many landmarks lie far off-axis across the image;
- the field of view is wide and features populate the periphery;
- the scene texture strongly constrains in-plane orientation;
- strong orientation priors are available;
- the optimizer is already initialized near the correct basin.

In those cases, optical-axis yaw can be observed quite well.

## Best Wording For The Project

So the safest wording is not:

```text
optical-axis yaw is always less reliable than tilt
```

but:

```text
in many forward-looking, center-dominated, or weakly initialized BA regimes,
the pose component around the viewing direction is less observable than tilt,
especially near the optical axis and under strong state coupling
```

That is the version most consistent with both:

- the repository geometry;
- and practical visual optimization behavior.

## Connection To The Repository Methods

This also explains the method split:

- `polar_cov_joint` is needed because raw `phi` can be unreliable near the optical axis;
- `polar_staged` is needed because in coupled BA the weakly observed optical-axis component can drag the full joint optimization into a wrong basin.

So these are two different layers of the same story:

- observation reliability;
- optimization robustness.

## Selected Q&A

### Q1

Does this argument depend on the equidistant projection model?

### A1

The exact formulas do, but the qualitative point is broader:

- spin around the viewing axis has very small image effect near the image center,
- while tilt still causes first-order displacement there.

That observation is not unique to equidistant projection.

### Q2

Does this prove that staged optimization is always necessary?

### A2

No.

It only explains why the staged story is geometrically plausible and why the repository uses large optical-axis-yaw error as a meaningful stress axis.
